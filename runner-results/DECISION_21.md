## Section A — The answer first

1. **A live Bal Harbour permit stays alive as long as it gets an approved inspection at least every 180 days.** Each approved inspection restarts the 180-day clock. That is why unit 220 lasted until 2026-08-16.
2. **Once it expires, there is a 180-day window to renew it on the same plans.** Inside that window, Bal Harbour gives a 50% credit on the permit fee. The Village form says: fees over $1,000 pay 50% plus county fees; fees under $1,000 pay in full plus county fees.
3. **After the 180-day window, the permit is not automatically dead.** The code says the building official **may** order the work removed, **or may** issue a new permit and require the work to meet the current code. It is the official's choice.
4. **On 9/9 the Village chose the gentle path for all four units.** Hebert Martinez wrote that the permits for 922, 321, 220 and 721 all show EXPIRED, so the application must be a **renewal, not an extension**. The Village will calculate renewal fees once the originals arrive, and issue the renewal once paid. **He did not say any unit needs a brand-new permit — including 922 and 321, which our 09-02 report had written off.**
5. **Bigger lever, not yet used: a state emergency may have paused the clock on 220 and 721.** Florida law (s. 252.363) pauses a building permit's clock during a Governor's natural-emergency declaration and then adds 24 months. A statewide emergency (EO 26-33) was in force from 2026-02-09, while 220 and 721 were still live. **If it applies, 220 and 721 may not be expired at all.** It needs a written notice to the Village within 90 days after the emergency ends. See Section D.

## Section B — What the code says, plainly

**Florida Building Code, 8th Edition (2023), Section 105.4.1 and its sub-sections** (Florida's own wording, which differs from the national model code):

1. **Active progress.** Work counts as active when the permit gets an approved inspection within 180 days. Miss that, and the permit expires.
2. **Expired permit, work started.** A new (renewed) permit for the same work must be obtained before work continues.
3. **The 180-day rule after expiry.** If a new permit is not obtained within 180 days of the date the permit became null and void, the building official is authorised to require the work be removed. **Alternatively**, the official may issue a new permit, requiring the work in place and the remaining work to meet the code in force.
4. **Fees.** The fee for renewal, reissue and extension is set by the local authority — in this case, the Village fee schedule.
5. **Extensions** must be asked for, in writing, **before** the permit expires. That is why the Village turned the 9/9 "extension" into a "renewal": all four were already expired.

**Bal Harbour's own rules:**

1. **Fee schedule item AD020, "Permit Renewals"** (FY 2025-26 schedule): where a permit has become null and void under the Florida Building Code, a 50% credit of the permit fee is applied to the re-application fee, for the same project and the same plans. No credit if a refund was already given. The search summary also reported a six-month limit on this credit; that wording was not seen directly.
2. **Village permit application form (Rev. 1/13/25)**, in the job capsule: has its own "Extension" and "Renewal" boxes, and says fees over $1,000 pay 50% plus county fees, under $1,000 pay in full plus county fees.
3. **Village building permits page:** an expired application filed before the 2023 code took effect (2023-12-31) could be extended on a written good-faith request within 180 days of expiry. Otherwise it needs a new process number, up-front fees and plans that meet the 2023 code. **This is about applications (process numbers), not issued permits, and its 180-day window has long passed.**
4. **Extension fee:** the 2022 schedule lists $150 per extension. The FY 2026 figure was not confirmed.

## Section D — The emergency pause (Florida Statute 252.363)

1. **The law.** When the Governor declares a state of emergency for a natural emergency, the time left on a building permit is paused for the length of the emergency, and then **24 more months** are added. Total cap: 48 months across several emergencies. Applies back to 2022-09-28. **The holder must notify the issuing authority in writing within 90 days after the emergency ends, naming the specific permit.**
2. **The emergency.** EO 26-33, signed 2026-02-09, covered **all 67 counties**, including Miami-Dade (cold front, 31 Jan – 3 Feb 2026). It was renewed by EO 26-80 (2026-04-10), EO 26-114 (2026-06-08, to 2026-08-07) and EO 26-163 (2026-08-06, 60 days, to about 2026-10-05). **No later renewal was found.**
3. **Why it matters here.** On 2026-02-09, unit 220 (expiring 2026-08-16) and unit 721 (issued 2026-01-15) were both **live**. If the statute applies, their clocks paused on 2026-02-09 and gain 24 months. **They might be extendable instead of renewable, which could avoid the renewal fee entirely.**
4. **The deadline.** If the emergency ended around 2026-10-05, the 90-day notice deadline is around **2027-01-03.**
5. **Units 922 and 321** were live during Hurricane Milton (EO 24-214, amended by EO 24-215 on 2024-10-05 to 51 counties; one secondary source lists Miami-Dade). The notice deadline for Milton has very likely passed. **Not claimed.**

**Not claimed:** that the Village accepts s. 252.363 for these permits; that EO 26-33 was not extended again; that the cold-front order counts as a "natural emergency" in the Village's view. **These need the Village (or a lawyer), in writing.**
