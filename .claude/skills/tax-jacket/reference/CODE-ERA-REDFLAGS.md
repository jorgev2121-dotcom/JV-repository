# CODE-ERA RED FLAGS — Forensic Due-Diligence Reference

**Purpose.** Year-thresholds for building a "when was it built / was it permitted /
does its era trigger a defective-material or code-compliance red flag" storyline.
Each item carries an approximate year, what it means for DD, a source, and a
confidence tag.

**Confidence tags**
- **CONFIRMED** — corroborated across multiple independent sources; the year is solid.
- **APPROX** — the era is well established but the exact boundary year is fuzzy or
  regionally variable; treat the year as a window, not a line.
- **UNCERTAIN** — could not verify to the level Jorge needs; flagged with `?`. Do NOT
  present as fact without a primary-source check.

this environment, so every item rests on cross-corroborated snippets, not full-text

## Section A — Defective / hazardous materials by era

### A1. Asbestos (residential building materials)
- **Years:** Heavy residential use **~1920–1980**, peak **~1950–late 1970s**.
  First EPA restriction (spray-applied fireproofing/insulation) **1973**; CPSC banned
  it in wall-patching compound and fake fireplace embers **1977**; EPA spray-on ban
  **1978**. The comprehensive 1989 EPA ban was **overturned in 1991** (5th Circuit),
  so many asbestos products stayed legal for decades. Chrysotile (the last type in
  active use) finally banned **March 18, 2024**.
- **DD meaning:** A home built or renovated **before ~1980** should be presumed to
  contain asbestos-containing materials (floor tile, mastic, popcorn ceiling, pipe
  wrap, siding, joint compound) until tested. There was never a clean "banned"
  cutoff — stockpiles were used into the 1980s.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED** (use "presume before ~1980"; avoid stating a single ban year).

### A2. Lead-based paint
- **Year:** Federal ban on lead paint for residential/consumer use **1978**
  (>0.06% lead by weight prohibited). Disclosure obligations flow from **Title X**
  (Residential Lead-Based Paint Hazard Reduction Act, **1992**).
- **DD meaning:** **Pre-1978** construction is the bright line — ~75% of pre-1978
  homes still contain some lead paint. The 1978 rule never required removal. Any
  pre-1978 sale/lease triggers federal disclosure. This is the single cleanest,
  most-cited year-threshold in the whole reference.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED.**

### A3. Polybutylene ("poly-B") water pipe
- **Years:** Installed **~1978–1995** (often cited "late 1970s to mid-1990s"),
  ~10 million U.S. homes. Production ceased **~1996**. Removed from new construction
  after **1995**.
- **Why it failed:** Chlorine/chloramine in municipal water degrades the pipe from
  the inside out; fittings crack and burst. Marketed to last ~30 years; many systems
  failed in as little as **~10 years**. Replaced by **CPVC and PEX** (PEX is the
  current standard).
- **Nuance to state honestly:** It was **removed from plumbing codes / discontinued,
  not a clean federal "ban."** Sources conflict on the word "banned." Say
  "discontinued and removed from code after 1995," not "federally banned."
- **DD meaning:** Gray (sometimes blue/black) plastic supply pipe in a **1978–1995**
  home is a known liability; often uninsurable or a forced re-pipe.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED** (era) / **APPROX** (the "ban" framing — prefer "discontinued").

### A4. Cast-iron drain/waste pipe (South Florida)
- **Years / failure age:** Standard DWV material in homes built through the **1970s**.
  Design life 25–50 yr generally, 40–60 yr nominal — **but in South Florida many fail
  at just 25–30 years** due to salt-rich soil, humidity, hard/acidic water and
  saltwater intrusion.
- **DD meaning:** **Any original cast iron in a pre-1975 South Florida home is past
  its failure window** and should be scoped/camera-inspected. This is a
  South-Florida-specific acceleration, not a national number.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED** (era + accelerated SoFla failure) / **APPROX** (exact failure age
  varies 25–50 yr).

### A5. Galvanized steel ("black pipe" is the plumber's slang) water supply
- **Years:** Standard residential water-supply material **~1930s–late 1960s**; not
  used in new construction since the **mid-1970s**. Lifespan 40–60 yr (avg ~50), but
  many begin corroding in **under 25 years**; rusts from the inside out, chokes flow
  and pressure.
- **DD meaning:** Galvanized supply in a pre-1970 home is at/past end of life; expect
  low pressure, rusty water, and imminent re-pipe. **Note terminology:** "black pipe"
  colloquially can mean uncoated black steel (gas) vs. galvanized (water) — confirm
  which before writing it into a report.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED** (era + lifespan) / **UNCERTAIN?** (the exact "black pipe" label
  Jorge uses — verify it means galvanized water line in the specific job).

### A6. Aluminum branch-circuit wiring
- **Years:** Single-strand (solid) aluminum branch wiring used **~1965–1973**
  (copper-price spike), ~2 million homes (some sources stretch the window to ~1975).
- **Why hazardous:** Aluminum expands/contracts/oxidizes differently than copper;
  connections loosen, overheat, arc. CPSC/Franklin Research: pre-1972 aluminum-wired
  homes are **~55x** more likely to reach "fire hazard conditions" at connections.
  Accepted fixes: full re-wire or **COPALUM crimp** connectors.
- **DD meaning:** A **1965–1973** build is the trigger to inspect for solid aluminum
  branch circuits — a named fire hazard, not cosmetic.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED.**

### A7. Federal Pacific (Stab-Lok) and Zinsco electrical panels
- **Years:** FPE Stab-Lok in homes built **~1950–1990**; Zinsco **~1963–1981**.
- **Why hazardous:** Breakers can fail to trip on overload/short. CPSC data: **85% of
  FPE double-pole and 39% of single-pole** breakers failed UL test criteria; a 2005
  NJ court found FPE fraudulently labeled breakers as meeting standards. Zinsco
  breakers can fuse to the bus bar and fail to trip. Recommended remedy: replace the
  whole panel.
- **DD meaning:** Presence of either brand is a stand-alone fire-safety red flag
  regardless of build year; most likely in the **1950–1990** (FPE) / **1963–1981**
  (Zinsco) windows.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED.**

### A8. Chinese / corrosive drywall (Florida epicenter)
- **Years:** Imported and installed **2001–2009** (import shortage); highest-risk
  installs **2005–2008**. ~100,000+ homes affected; **Florida had the most** (>60% of
  CPSC complaints).
- **Why hazardous:** Off-gasses sulfur compounds that corrode copper (pipes, wiring,
  A/C coils turn black/powdery), damage electronics/appliances, and raise health
  complaints — worse in hot, humid climates.
- **DD meaning:** A Florida home **built or remodeled 2001–2009** (especially
  2005–2008) is squarely in the window; check for blackened A/C coils, corroded
  ground wires, and the tell-tale sulfur odor.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED.**

## Section B — Flood elevation / FEMA (the "sunroom vs. enclosed patio" dodge)

### B1. Enclosures below Base Flood Elevation (BFE)
- **The rule (FEMA/NFIP):** Below an elevated building, enclosed areas are allowed
  **only** for parking, building access, or storage; must use **flood-damage-resistant
  materials**; and must have **flood openings (vents)** for automatic entry/exit of
  floodwater. Non-engineered openings: **≥1 sq in of net open area per sq ft of
  enclosed area**, **≥2 openings on ≥2 different walls**, bottom of opening **≤12 in
  above grade**, top **below BFE**. (FEMA Technical Bulletin 1.)
- **Conversion is a violation:** Turning a below-BFE enclosure into **finished/
  conditioned living space**, or blocking the flood vents, **violates the floodplain
  development permit.** Screened, open-wall enclosures below BFE are sometimes exempt;
  a **conditioned sunroom is not** — windows/prime doors make it "interior space."
- **The "sunroom" dodge (why it happens):** A rear patio sitting **below BFE with a
  step-down** cannot legally be finished living space. Calling it a **"sunroom"** (or
  leaving it labeled "screen/patio enclosure") is how an unpermitted below-BFE
  conversion is dressed up to avoid the elevation + vent + flood-material requirements.
  The forensic tell is a **step-DOWN** into a glassed rear room whose floor is below
  the main slab / below BFE, with no visible flood vents.
- **DD meaning:** A below-BFE rear enclosure finished as living space, or one missing
  code flood vents, is (a) likely unpermitted and (b) a live NFIP compliance and
  insurance problem. The name on the plans ("sunroom") does not change the elevation
  facts.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED** (FEMA vent/enclosure rules; the sunroom-relabel pattern is a
  widely documented practitioner observation rather than a single cited statute —
  frame it as "the recognized dodge," not a code citation).

## Section C — Bathroom / shower code evolution

### C1. Fixture clearances (the real "code," current values)
- **Water closet (toilet):** centerline **≥15 in** to any side wall/obstruction;
  **≥21 in** clear in front (residential). NKBA comfort recommendation is more
  generous (30 in front; 36x66 compartment) — that is guidance, not code.
- **Shower:** minimum interior **30 in x 30 in** (must fit a 30-in disc), ≥900 sq in;
  ceiling ≥6 ft 8 in over a 30-in-square area. NKBA comfort: 36 x 36.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED** (current fixture clearances). These are the numbers to measure
  against in the field.

### C2. There is no code "minimum room size" — layout is driven by clearances
- Modern plumbing code sets **no minimum bathroom floor area**; the room size is
  whatever the fixture clearances force. (Some jurisdictions, e.g. California, layer
  on a 30 sq ft / 5-ft-min-width rule — jurisdiction-specific, not universal.)
- **Tag: CONFIRMED.**

### C3. Historic "standard" bathroom sizes
- The traditional American full bath ran **~5x7 (35 sq ft) to 5x8 (40 sq ft)**, common
  in homes built **1950s–1990s**; older homes skew small. Standardized shower stalls
  (e.g. 32x32) emerged with the post-WWII housing boom.
- **The specific "5'x7' → 5'x7.5'" change Jorge cited: `?` UNCERTAIN.** Could not
  verify a documented code change from 5x7 to 5x7.5 in any source. What IS supported:
  the ~5x7/5x8 traditional footprint, and that room size is really governed by fixture
  clearances (C1), not a stated minimum. **Do not cite the 5x7.5 figure as code
  without a primary source.**
- **Tag: APPROX** (traditional 5x7–5x8 footprint) / **UNCERTAIN?** (the exact
  5x7→5x7.5 transition).

### C4. When showers became common
- Showers spread into standard homes with indoor plumbing and the **post-WWII boom**
  (late 1940s–1950s). No single "required by code" year surfaced; requirement is
  effectively "a bathing facility," with showers becoming the norm mid-century.
- **Tag: APPROX** (era) / **UNCERTAIN?** (a specific "showers required" code year —
  not found).

### C5. DD signal — the squeezed-vanity tell
- **What it means for DD:** An added or enlarged shower paired with a **crammed
  vanity or a toilet that no longer meets the 15-in centerline / 21-in front
  clearance** is a signature of an **unpermitted bathroom conversion/expansion** — the
  fixtures were shoehorned in without a plan review that would have enforced C1
  clearances. Measure the clearances; a failing clearance is objective evidence the
  work would not have passed permit.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED** (as an inspection heuristic tied to the confirmed C1 clearances).

## Section D — HVAC / central A/C and ductwork

### D1. Central A/C + ductwork era in residential South Florida
- **Years:** Central AC technology matured in the **1950s**; by the **1960s** most new
  Florida homes had central air (FL adoption ran well ahead of the U.S. — >50% by
  1970 vs. 36% nationally; >70% of Southern homes by 1975). Central AC with ducting
  became the **standard for new construction through the 1960s–1970s**.
- **DD meaning:** A South Florida home built from roughly the **1960s onward** would
  be expected to have been designed for central duct AC. A pre-1960 home was not, so
  any central system there is a **retrofit** — which is exactly where duct problems
  live.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED** (adoption era) / **APPROX** (no single "required by code" year
  for residential central AC surfaced — adoption was market-driven, then codified via
  energy code over time).

### D2. DD signal — "ducts just lying around / don't make sense"
- **What it means:** Disconnected, abandoned, or illogically routed ductwork
  typically indicates a **retrofit or an unpermitted addition/reconfiguration** —
  ducts run to serve a room that was added, or an old system was partially replaced
  without removing the previous runs. In a pre-1960s house it can mean a retrofit onto
  a building never designed for ducts; in any house it flags conditioned space that
  may have been added without a mechanical permit (and, in a flood zone, ties back to
  B1 — conditioned air into a below-BFE "sunroom").
- **Tag: CONFIRMED** (as an inspection heuristic) / interpretive, not a code citation.

## Section E — The grandfathering / "code at time of the infraction" rule

### E1. What the code actually says
- **Baseline:** An existing building is generally **grandfathered to the code in force
  when it was built** — repairs "shall not make the building less conforming" than it
  was before. This is the Florida Building Code, **Existing Building** (FEBC) regime.
- **Alterations trigger scrutiny by level (FEBC Ch. 6–9):**
  - **Level 1** — like-for-like replacement of materials/fixtures.
  - **Level 2** — reconfiguring space, adding/removing a door or window, extending a
    system, adding equipment.
  - **Level 3** — work area exceeds **50%** of the building; broadest compliance
    obligations.
  The bigger the alteration level, the more of the **current** code you must meet.
- **Legalizing unpermitted work (after-the-fact permit):** FBC allows retroactive
  permitting **provided the work meets code, is documented with as-builts, and passes
  inspection.** Miami-Dade/Broward after-the-fact permits carry a **higher fee
  multiplier (~2x–4x)** and more inspector discretion. If the as-built does not meet
  code, remediation is required before legalization.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED** (FEBC alteration-level structure; after-the-fact permit process).

### E2. The honest nuance on Jorge's phrasing
- Jorge's rule of thumb — **"if you touch/legalize it you must bring it to the code of
  the year of the infraction"** — captures the right instinct (once you disturb or
  legalize work, grandfathering protection is lost and code applies), but the precise
  legal mechanics are worth stating carefully:
  1. Untouched, permitted, code-at-the-time work stays **grandfathered**.
  2. When you **alter** it, the alteration must meet code per the **FEBC alteration
     level** (Level 1/2/3), and additions must meet **current** code.
  3. When you **legalize unpermitted work**, the after-the-fact permit is judged
     against the code the jurisdiction applies at review — in practice most Florida
     jurisdictions assess it against **current code** (or the code in effect when the
     work was performed, at the building official's discretion). "Code of the year of
     the infraction" is the conservative framing; some jurisdictions demand current
     code outright.
- **Practical takeaway that IS solid:** unpermitted or altered work loses
  grandfathering and must be brought up to code to be legalized — that is the
  load-bearing fact for a DD storyline.
- **Tag: CONFIRMED** (grandfathering lost on alteration/legalization) / **APPROX?**
  (the exact "year of the infraction" vs. "current code" question is
  jurisdiction- and official-dependent; verify with the Miami-Dade/relevant building
  department for a specific job).
